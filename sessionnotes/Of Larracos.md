I have been to Larracos on eleven separate occasions and I have never once left in the condition in which I arrived. On my seventh visit I woke up on a fishing boat halfway to the Dornbry coast with no memory of how I had come to be there and a substantial sum of money in my pocket that had not been there the previous evening. The captain, when I inquired, refused to discuss the matter and set me ashore at the next opportunity. I mention this by way of introduction.

Larracos is the second city of Balenthas. Thirty thousand souls, more or less, though I suspect the census does not include everyone it might, and I suspect further that a substantial number of the residents would prefer to remain uncensused. It sits at the point where the Southern Road runs out of land and pours itself into the Arlun. Every sack of Valdrek grain, every barrel of Valdrek wine, every hide, every wheel of cheese from the Kettl foothills that has traveled south — all of it comes through Larracos. And nearly all of it leaves again, on ships bound for places its farmers have never heard of.

The imperial Larracos, my elders tell me, was a tidy little town. A customs house. Some warehouses. An aqueduct, still standing, which the current inhabitants use as a public bath and about which the less said the better. When the empire fell, most of the elven administrators packed up or died, but the road remained, and the grain remained, and the ships remained, and so Larracos remained also, and then Larracos began to grow into whatever it has since become, which is difficult to describe and impossible to govern.

The city has no walls. I have raised this with several municipal officials over the years and the response is always the same, delivered with a shrug: what would be the point? Larracos has never been besieged in living memory. When trouble has come from the sea — and it has, more than once, in the form of raiders or ambitious pirate captains or, on one particularly memorable occasion, an actual dragon — the response has always been the same. Pay them. Hire them. Feed them. Talk them out of it over dinner. I am given to understand that the dragon was talked out of it, though the story I heard involved a very specific type of cheese and I have never been able to confirm the details.

You arrive by the Southern Road and the first thing you notice is the smell. I say this with affection. Larracos smells of fish and tar and salt and pitch and frying meat and, depending on the wind, the tanneries, which I do not recommend visiting. You cannot see the sea at first because the warehouses are in the way. They stack three and four stories tall behind the docks and they are, most of them, full of grain from farms whose owners will never see this city. There is a moment when you walk between them — the tall wooden walls on either side, the smell of salt getting stronger, the seagulls arguing overhead — and then you turn a corner and the harbor opens up in front of you, and it is, I confess, one of my favorite views in the known world.

The harbor is always crowded. I mean this literally. I once, on a wager with a Balenthasi dockworker whose name I have unfortunately forgotten, walked across the harbor at low tide by stepping from deck to deck. It took me an hour and I fell in twice. I won the wager. I do not recommend the exercise but I do recommend the drink that was purchased with the proceeds. Something in Larracos called *ossuvine*, distilled locally, clear as water and roughly three times as dangerous. The stills of Larracos are famous throughout the Arlun. There are, I am told, small pirate crews who sail here specifically to resupply their onboard distilleries, though this may be one of those stories that Larracos tells about itself for the pleasure of the telling. Larracos does that. You learn to check.

Behind the harbor, behind the warehouses, the city itself sprawls in whatever direction it felt like sprawling. There is no plan. There has never been a plan. The streets go where the streets go and if you ask for directions you will receive them in a system of landmarks that includes at least three taverns, a fountain that has not run in a decade, and a particular tree. I have never become truly lost in Larracos but I have never been entirely certain where I was, either, and I have come to believe these are the same condition.

The Great Market is where three streets meet, though whether the streets meet because the market is there or the market is there because the streets meet is a question I have put to several local historians and received no satisfactory answer. You can buy anything there. I mean this. On my last visit I saw a man selling live crabs from a hat. The hat was still on his head. The crabs seemed to have made their peace with this arrangement. I also, on the same visit, saw a woman selling love charms she had made that morning from what looked to me like ordinary river stones and some string, and doing brisk business at three copper apiece. I bought one to see what would happen. Nothing has happened yet. I remain hopeful.

The Salt Quarter is where you go when you have finished with the market and are ready to become someone else for a while. Taverns, one against the next, sometimes two deep. Music from every doorway, at every hour. I have visited the Salt Quarter at dawn on a Tuesday and there was music playing. I have visited at the third hour after midnight in the middle of winter and there was music playing. I have come to believe that if the music ever stopped, something in the arrangement of the city would come undone, and so the people of Larracos ensure, without ever having discussed the matter, that it never does. The Salt Quarter is where the pirates drink. I mean this openly. Not the great pirate captains — those are down in the Grimhold shallows or up the Dunharg coast, and they do not visit Larracos except very quietly. But the small pirates, the two-ship crews, the seasonal raiders, the men and women who take a bit of cargo here and a bit of cargo there — they drink in the Salt Quarter and nobody troubles them. I once spent an evening at a table with a woman named Kessa Vane who had, that very morning, apparently taken a Valdrek merchantman by boarding action in full sight of the harbor. She bought me three drinks and told me about her mother. I have not yet decided what to make of Larracos on that basis.

The Lantern Row runs alongside the Salt Quarter and blurs into it at several points, in a manner that respectable maps decline to acknowledge. Small red-glassed lanterns outside each establishment. The workers there are organized into what functions as a guild, with elected representatives who sit — and I stress that I am not inventing this — on certain municipal commissions. Visitors from Valdrek find this scandalous. Visitors from Dunharg find it charming. The Balenthasi consider it simply good governance and I am, upon reflection, inclined to agree. The workers of Lantern Row know more about what is happening in this city than anyone else, and I have found that standing a drink for one of them is a more reliable source of information than any number of official inquiries. I mention this for scholarly purposes only.

There is a harbormaster, a man called Corvis Wend, who has held the post for twenty-three years and has, in that time, cultivated an ignorance of pirate activity that I regard as one of the great artistic achievements of the Arlun basin. I have shared several bottles with Corvis and he is, in private, an extremely well-informed man. In public he sees nothing. He is paid, I believe, quite well by parties on both sides of this arrangement, and I have never held it against him. Larracos would not work without him. That is worth something.

The Municipal Council of nine members governs Larracos, allegedly. It is appointed by some combination of trade guild elections, hereditary succession, and what I have been repeatedly told is a lottery, though the mechanics of this lottery have never been adequately explained to me and I have begun to suspect the lottery is a polite fiction covering something else. The Council is theoretically subordinate to the Merchant Council in Selevast, and in practice governs itself, and Selevast permits this because Larracos generates more tax revenue than any three other cities combined and because attempting to reform Larracos would, I suspect, result in Larracos quietly and cheerfully seceding within a fortnight. There is a mayor. I have never learned the mayor's name. Nobody I have asked has learned the mayor's name. I have come to believe this is intentional.

The patron god of Larracos is Ossuvar, who is an aspect of the Watcher Below adopted by the city as its own. His temple sits at the end of the main pier and is decorated, in what I consider a magnificent piece of civic honesty, with the bones of great sea creatures. His priests are famously accepting of pirates, drunks, prostitutes, smugglers, wayward scholars, and anyone else who has ever done something they would prefer not to explain in detail. The philosophy of Ossuvar's clergy is that the sea takes everyone in the end and there is no point being fussy about who visits the god's hall in the meantime. I have spent many good evenings in that temple. The wine is better than it has any right to be.

I will admit that Larracos should not work. By every principle of political theory I have ever encountered, this city should be a disaster. And yet the ships come in and the goods go out and the taxes are collected and the children grow up and the old people die, mostly peacefully, and the music never stops. I have been trying to understand Larracos for eleven visits now and I do not understand it any better than I did the first time. I suspect this is the point. I intend to return next spring.
