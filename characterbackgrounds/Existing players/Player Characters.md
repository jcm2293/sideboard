### Player Characters
## Stookin starting level 3 barbarian path of the ancestors 
Stookin H. Gear, the Awakened
Stookin H. Gear has lived in Pellvar his entire life. Pellvar, as you may have read in the histories, is a quarry town of perhaps two hundred souls in western Valdrek, whose chief industry is cutting stone from the ruins of an empire that died nearly two hundred years ago and selling that stone to people who can afford nicer buildings than the Pellvarian quarrymen themselves will ever own. It is, in the kind words of the elven scholar Tavar the Unabridged, "a backwater of the first order." Tavar means this affectionately. The Pellvarians, when this assessment was relayed to them, did not.
Stookin's family is one of the older ones in town. His great-grandfather quarried stone. His grandfather quarried stone. His father quarries stone. His older brothers quarry stone. The plan was for Stookin to also quarry stone. This plan has not survived contact with Stookin.
The thing about Stookin — and the thing that has, in the kindest possible reading, baffled his family — is that he has always had what he describes, when pressed, as "the big feelings." He cannot quarry stone, because he stops to look at the patterns in the rock and feels something. He cannot drive the carts, because the horses make him feel something. He cannot work the inn, because the customers make him feel something. He has spent the better part of his late adolescence and early adulthood — Stookin is roughly twenty-one, though he has never been entirely sure — lying on the cot in the back of his family's home, staring at the ceiling beams, eating the smoked goose tendons that the Pellvar butcher salts and dries by the bushel for the quarrymen to chew on during their breaks.
His family has, with great patience, attempted on multiple occasions to determine what is wrong with Stookin. The visiting healer from Balenthas suggested melancholia. The hedge witch from up the road suggested an excess of yellow bile. The local priest of Maren the Hearthkeeper suggested he simply needed to find a nice girl, a piece of advice his mother has not stopped repeating in the four years since it was given. Stookin himself, when asked, has only ever been able to say that he feels very large things, and that nobody else seems to feel them, and that this fact is itself one of the things he feels very largely about.
What Stookin did not know — what nobody in Pellvar knew, because nobody alive has the context for what was happening to him — is that he was waking up.
	You may have read the histories. For eight hundred years under the elven empire, natural magic was identified in childhood and gently, exquisitely, politely suffocated. People born with talent were trained out of it. Whole bloodlines were quietly redirected. The result was a world where magic existed but only inside imperial systems, run by imperial mages, in service of imperial purposes. And then the empire fell, and the suppression ended, and across the surviving lands of Alathren, in farmhouses and fishing villages and quarry towns, people started waking up to gifts that had been sleeping in their families for generations.
Stookin's gift is older than most.
About six months ago, on what he insists was an entirely ordinary night, Stookin had a dream. In the dream, his ancestors — his true ancestors, he says, and refuses to clarify what he means by "true" — gathered around him in a great circle and spoke to him in a language he had never heard but understood entirely. They told him that things were not supposed to be the way they were. That the world had been broken once and was being broken again, in slower and quieter ways. That it could heal. That it had to get worse first, but then it could heal. They showed him things he could not afterward describe. He woke up in a cold sweat, threw on his furs, ran to the town square ahead of the roosters, and proclaimed — to the half-asleep night watchman and the baker who had been up since three — that things are not supposed to remain as they are, they can get better, they have to get worse first, but then they can get better, the world can heal and so can we.
We will, out of consideration for those with weak stomachs, spare you the precise details of what happened next. It is sufficient to note that Pellvar is a town where the watchman is allowed to use his cudgel at his own discretion, and that Stookin's mother had to explain to several neighbors over the following week that her son had simply had a bad dream and would not be doing it again, and that no, he had not been into the brandy, and yes, she was very embarrassed.
Stookin has, in fact, been doing it again. He has had three more visions since that night. He no longer announces them in the town square. He has learned this much. But when he is alone in the quarry-fields at the edge of town, where the half-buried stones of some old elven structure jut out of the heather, he sometimes hears his ancestors clearly enough that he answers them out loud. He has begun to understand what they want from him. He is not entirely sure he is up to it. He is, however, ready to leave Pellvar for the first time in his life, because his ancestors have made it clear that whatever he is supposed to do, he cannot do it from his cot, eating tendons.
He does not know what kind of ancestors he is hearing. He has not thought to ask. We — and by "we" I mean you, the reader, who has read the histories Stookin has not — might wonder whether they are the elven dead of Mordendal, whose blood may have entered Pellvar through some refugee who passed through after the Fall, and gone silent for two centuries. We might wonder whether they are spirits of the land itself, one of Tavar's "little gods," roused by something in Stookin's particular blood. We might wonder whether they are something older, and stranger, and we are right to wonder. Stookin does not wonder. Stookin just listens.

## Ren Starting level 3 Gloomstalker ranger
Player character brief — for DM use

Florentina Meadows
Called Ren. Do not call her Florentina.

I should note, before I begin, that I am writing this entry not from any great scholarly interest in the girl herself — she is seventeen, and seventeen is not an age that warrants scholarship — but because she is the most interesting thing in Pellvar, which is an admittedly modest distinction. She accosted me outside the inn on my second morning here, having apparently decided that a traveling elven scholar was exactly what she had been waiting for, though she expressed this conviction through a scowl and a series of pointed questions rather than anything so undignified as enthusiasm.

ORIGINS

Her people are sheep farmers of the sort that Valdrek produces in considerable quantity: sturdy, contented, and deeply unbothered by the ruins they live beside. There are ten sisters in the Meadows household — Rose, Lily, Violet, and so forth, in a naming tradition that one suspects exhausted itself by the time it reached its youngest subject, who arrived in the world as Florentina and has been declining the name ever since. The sisters are, by all accounts, delightful. They help with the lambing. They have opinions about wool. Ren regards them with the particular horror one reserves for a life narrowly escaped.

CHARACTER

She wears exclusively black, which in a quarry town requires considerable ingenuity and, I suspect, several creative applications of boot dye. She darkens the skin beneath her eyes with quarry dust to achieve an effect of dramatic exhaustion, despite sleeping, by her own reluctant admission during a moment of honesty she immediately regretted, extremely well. Every morning she walks to the quarry and throws rocks back into it — a gesture of protest against the dismantling of ruins she considers sacred, which would be more effective if anyone noticed — and then sits on an old foundation stone and casts her words into the air for the crows. She tells me this is a ritual. The crows, for their part, do occasionally answer. She has not mentioned this.

ABILITIES

She has trained herself, through sheer stubbornness and without instruction, to move through the ruins at night in ways that have no natural explanation. The empire's suppression of natural gifts lasted eight centuries, but it is over now, and gifts will out. Hers emerged not through accident or pact but through the particular magic of a person who has decided something is true about themselves and refused to be argued out of it. I find this, in a young human, genuinely admirable. I have not told her so, as she would find it insufferable coming from an elf.

A NOTE FOR THE DM

She knows something is wrong with the statues. She has known longer than anyone. She is desperate to be taken seriously about it and constitutionally incapable of asking to be. She is, underneath the quarry dust and the scowling, a remarkably sound sleeper — and I believe, though I would not say so to her face, that the world is about to become exactly as interesting as she always suspected it was.

— Tavar the Unabridged, writing from Pellvar, western Valdrek

## Mara #starting level 3 rogue investigator 
Mara Thornlow is twenty-three and grew up on the edge of Pellvar, in a small stone cottage her parents built themselves where the cleared paths give way to scrub and then, eventually, to the genuine forest that runs west toward the Dornbry. Her parents are quiet people — her mother weaves, her father does mid-skill stonework on commission rather than in the quarry pits — and they raised her without much ceremony or fuss. Which suited her fine, because Mara, from the time she could walk, did not particularly want ceremony or fuss. She wanted to be outside. She wanted to know what the birds were doing. She wanted to follow the deer trail behind the cottage and see where it went, and when her parents told her not to, she would nod and agree and then do it anyway. The town has a word for people like her, which is "unusual," and they say it without affection or unkindness, the way they would describe the weather.
She knows the boys, of course — Pellvar is small, and you cannot avoid knowing everyone — but she has never quite been part of their orbit. She knows Rowan because everyone knows Rowan. She knows Donovan because she has, on three separate occasions, returned a dog he'd lost — Dante is bad at staying where he's put, and Mara is good at finding things, which is how that triangle has worked for years. She knows Stookin because she was the only one in the town square the morning of his vision who listened to what he was saying instead of how he was saying it, and quietly took notes on a piece of birch bark she had in her pocket. She still has those notes. She has not shown them to anyone. The boys think of her, when they think of her at all, as "that quiet one who lives near the woods," which is fine with Mara, who has never particularly wanted to be thought of more than that.
What she does, mostly, is notice things. She notices that the bark on the western quarry-face has been scored in a pattern that doesn't match any tool she's ever seen. She notices that the deer trails near the old elven ruins shift each spring in ways that suggest something is moving them deliberately. She notices that the elven scholar currently staying at the inn is writing in his ledger every time someone uses a particular phrase, and she has begun keeping her own list of what those phrases are. She is not a trained investigator — Pellvar does not have trained investigators. She is simply a young woman with an excellent memory, an unhurried mind, and a deep instinctive sense that something here is not what people are saying it is. She doesn't yet know what. She has a feeling she is about to find out.

## Donovan starting level three Bard, COllege of glamour 
Donovan, the Sad-Boy of Pellvar
Donovan was born to sheep farmers of mostly human origin, on a hilly plot of grazing land about two hours' walk west of Pellvar, where the heather gives way to scrub and the old imperial road bends toward Balenthas. The Pellvar shepherds are particularly skilled, but they produce a passable wool that gets sold through the Pellvar markets twice a year, and they have done this, mostly without incident and largely without ambition, for as long as anyone can remember. Donovan was supposed to be a shepherd. Donovan, when asked what HE wants to be, will look out at the middle distance and say "a voice" in a tone that suggests he has rehearsed this answer in a mirror.
Donovan identifies deeply with his Mother's distant Elvish roots. His mother has always told him he is 1/6 Elvish, and he likes to tell the tales of the great Fall of Mordendal as if they happened to him directly.The ballad he opens most performances with concerns the burning of Mordendal and the grief of its survivors, which Donovan delivers as though he personally walked out of those forests with ash on his cheeks. He was not alive for this. The Fall was one hundred and eighty-seven years ago. Donovan is twenty-two. 

The defining event of his life so far happened five summers ago, when a half-elf woman named Elowynth passed through the village and took his virginity in the loft above his family's barn. Half-elves are not unheard of in this region but they are not common — most travel through Balenthas, and most do not stop in a place like Pellvar — so her arrival was, by local standards, an event. She was traveling somewhere. He never quite understood where. She stayed three nights. She had silver in her hair and a way of laughing that made him feel, for the first time in his seventeen years, seen. On the third morning she kissed him on the forehead, said something in Elvish that he did not understand and has spent the last five years trying to translate, and rode south. She has not, to anyone's knowledge, ever returned to Pellvar. She also, to be perfectly clear, almost certainly does not remember Donovan's name. Each ballad he sings is for her.

Donovan has, since that summer, become the village's resident sad-boy blues singer, performing in the Pellvar inn most evenings in a signature double act with his small singing dog Dante. Dante is some kind of brown spaniel mix and was, in fact, found shivering at the edge of the road the morning after Elowynth left, which Donovan interprets as a sign and which the rest of the town interprets as Donovan's mother getting tired of his moping and finally letting him have a dog. Dante howls along when Donovan plays the harmonica. Donovan and Dante share the same big brown sad eyes–the eyes which are a siren song to women. Donovan is, against every reasonable expectation of his upbringing, deeply hot. You can't look away when Donovan performs: you want to be him, and fix him, and hold him, and be chosen by him. Bess from the inn is in love with him. Several married women in the surrounding farmsteads come to the inn every tuesday night but tell their husbands they are in a knitting circle. 

He leaves a trail of broken hearts wherever he goes, but always feels he is the victim. He has never, not once in his twenty-two years, considered the possibility that he might be the problem. He dreams of being the most loved boy in the whole wide world.

What Donovan does not yet know — what no one in Pellvar yet knows — is that the kind of magic he keeps producing accidentally during performances is not a normal thing. The empire suppressed natural magic for eight hundred years, and since the Fall it has been waking back up in random people across Alathren, often in ways those people don't recognize as magic at all. Donovan thinks he is "really feeling it tonight" when the room goes quiet and Bess starts crying. Donovan thinks he is just "a powerful performer" when a tough-looking quarryman gets misty during the second verse and buys a round for the table. Donovan does not realize that the small enchantments he is weaving are real, that they are reaching out and making people feel things, that what flows between him and his audience is not just emotion but bardic glamour — the oldest and most quietly powerful form of magic the post-imperial world has begun to remember. He is, in the language Tavar would use, a wildflower that has not yet noticed it is blooming. He thinks he is just sad. He thinks he is just talented. He is also right about both of those things, but he is, importantly, a third thing as well, and he has no idea.
